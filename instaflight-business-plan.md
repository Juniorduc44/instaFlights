# InstaFlight
### The Membership Aggregator for Private Aviation
**Business Plan — Draft v1.0**

---

## Executive Summary

InstaFlight is an asset-light membership aggregator for private aviation. It owns no aircraft, employs no pilots, and runs no maintenance operations. Instead, it owns the **customer relationship**: members pay InstaFlight a single subscription or membership fee, and InstaFlight negotiates access on their behalf across a network of existing private aviation providers (fractional operators, jet-card programs, and charter operators).

The analogy is direct: **Instacart doesn't own Costco's warehouses — it owns the customer relationship and resells access at scale.** InstaFlight applies the same logic to private aviation, where today's customer has to individually evaluate, negotiate with, and commit to operators like NetJets, Flexjet, VistaJet, or Wheels Up, each with different pricing structures, contract terms, and minimum commitments.

This is deliberately the **lower-risk, faster-to-launch** of the two concepts in this strategy (compare to the companion Aviation Mutual plan). It requires no aircraft capital, no FAA operating certificate of its own, and can generate membership revenue from day one.

---

## 1. The Problem

Private aviation access today requires the customer to do all the comparison work themselves, across fundamentally different pricing models:

- **Fractional ownership** (NetJets, Flexjet): six-figure share purchase, five-figure monthly management fees, multi-year contracts
- **Membership/jet card** (Wheels Up, VistaJet Program): large prepaid deposits, annual fees, per-hour charges
- **On-demand charter**: no membership, but no volume pricing either — pay full retail per flight

As one industry comparison put it plainly: <cite index="10-1">the jet card locks in a prepayment, the membership commits you for several years with a minimum number of hours, and fractional ownership combines a share purchase with non-negotiable monthly fees</cite> — <cite index="13-1">getting the model wrong means either tying up hundreds of thousands of dollars for underused access, or paying full retail flight-by-flight when volume would have justified a smoothed rate</cite>.

Most private-aviation customers are not sophisticated enough (or don't want to spend the time) to model this correctly. That's the opening for an aggregator.

## 2. The Model

### 2.1 What InstaFlight Does
- Negotiates enterprise/volume memberships or corporate contracts directly with private aviation operators
- Resells access to individual members under a single, simplified InstaFlight membership
- Handles booking, member service, and billing through one platform/app
- Captures the spread between its negotiated volume rate and the price members pay

### 2.2 What the Member Experiences
The member only interacts with InstaFlight:
- One app, one subscription, one bill
- Reserve a seat or a flight; InstaFlight handles which underlying operator fulfills it
- No need to compare NetJets vs. Wheels Up vs. VistaJet contract terms — InstaFlight already did that

### 2.3 Why Operators Would Participate
Operators gain a **customer acquisition channel** without their own sales/marketing cost:
- Instead of acquiring thousands of individual retail customers, they fill capacity through one wholesale relationship
- This is the same logic operators already use with charter brokers — <cite index="13-1">broker-based jet cards already source aircraft from a wide operator network in exchange for buying flexibility and aircraft choice for the customer</cite> — InstaFlight is a membership-oriented version of that broker relationship, aimed at recurring subscription customers rather than one-off bookings

### 2.4 Revenue Model
- Monthly or annual member subscription fee
- Margin between InstaFlight's negotiated wholesale/volume rate and the retail-equivalent price charged to members
- Potential tiered membership levels (e.g., number of flight hours or booking priority included)

## 3. Learning From Comparable Companies

### 3.1 Charter brokers — the closest existing analog
On-demand charter brokers already do a simplified version of this: <cite index="13-1">broker programs source aircraft from a wide operator network, buying the customer flexibility and aircraft choice, with strong brokers offsetting cabin-consistency variability through operator vetting and clear substitution language</cite>. This validates the core mechanic (aggregating operator capacity behind one customer relationship) but brokers today are typically transactional, not subscription-based. InstaFlight's innovation is making that aggregation a **recurring membership product**, not a one-off booking service.

### 3.2 Wheels Up — membership without full ownership, but still capital-heavy
Wheels Up shows both the opportunity and the trap. It succeeded in building <cite index="12-1">a membership model where members access aircraft through a mix of owned fleet, fractional aircraft, and charter partners</cite> — proving customers will pay a subscription for simplified access. But it still owned a meaningful chunk of its own fleet, which meant it carried real capital exposure, and it needed <cite index="9-1">a Delta-led recapitalization to stabilize</cite> when the model came under financial pressure.

**Implication for InstaFlight:** stay fully asset-light. InstaFlight's entire value proposition is *not* owning aircraft — the moment it starts owning or leasing planes directly to guarantee capacity, it inherits Wheels Up's capital structure risk without Wheels Up's fleet scale to absorb it.

### 3.3 Surf Air — the danger of trying to do too much at once
Surf Air's <cite index="5-1">decade of shifting between owning its own aircraft, an asset-light outsourced model, and even a blockchain loyalty program</cite> is a warning about scope creep. InstaFlight should resist the temptation to vertically integrate (buy aircraft, hire pilots) even after it has scale — that's a different, much harder business (see the companion Aviation Mutual plan for that path).

### 3.4 Costco/Instacart — the aggregator playbook that actually works at scale
This is the cleanest structural analog: a low-margin, high-trust membership seller (Costco) has its warehouse relationship intermediated by a convenience-focused reseller (Instacart) that doesn't own inventory or stores. Instacart's value is entirely in demand aggregation, UX, and logistics — not asset ownership. InstaFlight should hold itself to the same discipline: the product is trust, simplicity, and negotiating leverage, not aircraft.

## 4. Competitive Positioning

| | Owns aircraft? | Member commitment | Core value prop |
|---|---|---|---|
| NetJets / Flexjet | Yes | 6-figure buy-in, multi-year | Guaranteed access, largest fleets |
| Wheels Up | Partial (owned + partner fleet) | Annual fee + deposit | Lower commitment than fractional |
| Charter brokers | No | None (transactional) | One-off flexibility |
| **InstaFlight** | **No** | **Simple recurring membership** | **One membership, every operator, no comparison-shopping** |

InstaFlight's edge is **simplicity and negotiating leverage on behalf of the member**, not price leadership — it cannot beat the wholesale rates operators would offer a true large-scale buyer, so its pitch to members is convenience and aggregated access, and its pitch to operators is guaranteed volume.

## 5. Financial Model — Assumptions & Structure

> Planning assumptions only; to be validated with real operator contract terms.

### 5.1 Revenue
- Member subscription (monthly/annual), tiered by expected usage/priority
- Spread captured between InstaFlight's negotiated operator rate and price charged to members

### 5.2 Costs
| Cost category | Notes |
|---|---|
| Operator contracts / volume commitments | Core cost; may require InstaFlight to guarantee minimum volume to unlock wholesale pricing |
| Platform/booking technology | App, scheduling, payments |
| Member service/support | Concierge-style service is part of the value prop |
| Sales/BD (operator relationships) | Ongoing negotiation and relationship management with operator partners |
| Marketing/member acquisition | Standard subscription-business CAC |

### 5.3 Key Risk to the Revenue Model
Operators only offer meaningful volume discounts once InstaFlight can deliver real, predictable volume. In the early stage, InstaFlight may not have enough members to negotiate wholesale rates — meaning early revenue may run on thinner (or negative) margin until scale is reached. This should be explicitly modeled and reserved for.

### 5.4 Unit Economics to Validate Before Launch
- Minimum member count needed to unlock a meaningful volume discount from at least one operator
- Member acquisition cost vs. lifetime membership value
- Whether the margin captured (wholesale-to-retail spread) covers platform + support costs at realistic membership sizes

## 6. Risks

| Risk | Why it matters | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Chicken-and-egg volume problem** | Need members to get operator discounts; need discounts to attract members | Launch with a small set of anchor operator relationships at modest volume commitments; grow both sides in parallel |
| **Operator disintermediation** | Operators could eventually court InstaFlight's members directly | Structure contracts so the member relationship (data, billing, app) stays with InstaFlight; make InstaFlight's UX/service the reason members stay |
| **Thin/negative early margin** | Real wholesale pricing power takes real scale | Model and reserve for a multi-quarter ramp before margin turns healthy |
| **Service quality variability** | Different underlying operators = inconsistent member experience | Vet operators carefully, as strong charter brokers already do; set clear service-level agreements |
| **Regulatory ambiguity** | InstaFlight is a reseller, not an operator — contracts must be structured so it isn't inadvertently acting as an unlicensed air carrier | Aviation regulatory counsel review before signing operator contracts |

## 7. Phased Roadmap

### Phase 1 — Launch the Membership Layer
- No aircraft, no pilots, no maintenance
- Sign initial operator partnerships (even at modest volume) to have real inventory to offer
- Build the booking/member app
- Generate recurring subscription revenue

### Phase 2 — Scale Negotiating Leverage
- Grow membership to unlock better wholesale rates
- Expand operator partnerships and aircraft categories available to members
- Improve margin as scale increases

### Phase 3 — Strategic Optionality
Once InstaFlight has real member volume and cash flow, two paths open up (not mutually exclusive):
- Continue as a pure aggregator, deepening operator relationships and margin
- Use accumulated member demand data to justify the Aviation Mutual's first aircraft purchase (see companion plan) — at that point, InstaFlight can also serve as the demand-filling layer for the Mutual's underutilized seats, without either entity compromising its core structure

## 8. Open Questions to Resolve Before Launch

1. Which operators (fractional, jet-card, and charter) are realistic first partners, and what volume commitment would they require for preferred pricing?
2. What legal structure keeps InstaFlight clearly a reseller/membership organization rather than an unlicensed air carrier?
3. What membership price point is competitive with going direct to an operator, while still preserving margin?
4. What's the minimum viable operator network needed at launch to make the membership credible (geographic coverage, aircraft categories)?
5. How does InstaFlight prevent members from being poached directly by operators once introduced?
