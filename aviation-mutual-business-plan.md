# The Aviation Mutual
### A Member-Owned Private Aviation Cooperative
**Business Plan — Draft v1.0**

---

## Executive Summary

The Aviation Mutual is a member-owned aviation cooperative that gives its members access to private air travel at cost, rather than at a profit-driven markup. Members pay a recurring subscription into a shared operating pool. That pool funds aircraft, crew, maintenance, insurance, and administration. There are no shareholders extracting profit — any operating surplus is reinvested into more aircraft, lower member costs, or expanded routes.

Structurally, it borrows from three proven models:

- **Credit unions** — member-owned, not-for-profit, surplus returned to members
- **Costco / warehouse clubs** — membership fee funds low-margin, high-volume access
- **Regional subscription air travel (Surf Air)** — scheduled point-to-point flying on a fixed membership fee

The mutual does **not** attempt to become a commercial airline or compete on price with commercial carriers. It competes with fractional ownership (NetJets, Flexjet) and jet-card/membership charter (Wheels Up, VistaJet) by removing the profit margin those companies build in for shareholders, and by aligning member incentives with cost control rather than growth-at-all-costs.

This document lays out the model, the operating plan, what similar companies got right and wrong, the financial assumptions, the risks, and a realistic path to launch.

---

## 1. The Problem

Private aviation today is priced for two extremes:

| Access model | Entry cost | Who it's for |
|---|---|---|
| Full ownership | $2M–$80M+ | Ultra-high-net-worth individuals/companies |
| Fractional ownership (NetJets, Flexjet) | ~$850K+ share purchase, $12K–$28K/month management fee, $8,500+/flight hour | High-frequency flyers (50–400 hrs/year) |
| Jet card / membership (Wheels Up, VistaJet Program) | $100K–$300K prepaid deposit, $6K–$8K/flight hour | Moderate, recurring flyers who want lower commitment |
| Charter (on-demand) | Pay per flight, no membership | Occasional flyers |

In every one of these models, a for-profit operator is pricing in a margin on top of the true cost of flying — because the operator, its investors, and (in several cases) a parent company's balance sheet all need a return. The Aviation Mutual's premise is simple: **remove the profit motive and the margin disappears, or gets reinvested into the members instead.**

---

## 2. What "Mutual" Means Here (and Why It's Not New)

The mutual/cooperative structure already works at scale in adjacent industries:

- **Credit unions**: member-owned deposit institutions where surplus is returned as lower fees/higher rates, not shareholder dividends.
- **Health-sharing ministries and mutual insurers** (e.g., State Farm's original mutual structure, USAA): members pool contributions; the pool pays claims; surplus is retained or returned to members.
- **Agricultural cooperatives** (e.g., Ocean Spray, Land O'Lakes): members jointly own processing and distribution infrastructure they couldn't afford individually.

Aviation has **no equivalent at meaningful scale today**. Flying clubs exist, but they're small, single-aircraft, single-airport operations — closer to a shared hangar than a real transportation network. The Aviation Mutual is that same flying-club logic, deliberately scaled with cooperative governance and professional operations.

## 3. How It Works

### 3.1 Membership & Funding
- Members pay a **monthly or annual subscription** into a shared operating pool.
- The pool is the sole source of funds for the organization — there is no external profit extraction.
- Membership tiers can differ by flight volume/priority (see §3.4), but **all tiers fund the same shared pool** — there is no separate "investor" class with different economic rights.

### 3.2 What the Pool Pays For
- Aircraft acquisition or lease payments
- Fuel
- Maintenance (scheduled and unscheduled)
- Hangar and airport fees
- Insurance
- Pilot and mechanic salaries
- Flight operations and scheduling staff
- Administrative overhead

### 3.3 Surplus Handling
Any surplus at period end is allocated, by a governance vote or a pre-set policy, to one or more of:
1. Reserve fund (required — see §6 on why this is non-negotiable)
2. Fleet expansion
3. Member cost reduction (rebate or lower next-period dues)
4. New route/destination development

### 3.4 Booking Priority
Rather than every member owning a fractional share of a specific tail number (the NetJets model), members reserve seats on a **shared flight calendar**. Flights are scheduled based on:
- Recurring/known demand routes
- Pre-committed advance bookings that hit a minimum load threshold
- Membership tier (higher-tier members may get earlier booking windows, not more ownership)

This is closer to how Surf Air scheduled flights on fixed regional routes than to fractional-share ownership — but unlike Surf Air, there's no shareholder capturing the margin between subscription revenue and operating cost.

### 3.5 Governance
Cooperative structures need real governance, not just marketing language:
- A member-elected board (or member-representative board in early stages, transitioning to elected as membership scales)
- Transparent, audited financials published to members
- Clear bylaws on surplus allocation, dues increases, and capital calls
- A professional management team runs day-to-day operations; the board sets policy and approves major capital decisions (e.g., aircraft purchases)

## 4. Learning From Comparable Companies

### 4.1 Surf Air — the closest analog, and a cautionary tale
Surf Air (founded 2013) built a subscription "all-you-can-fly" regional air model, initially <cite index="8-1">owning its own fleet of Pilatus PC-12 turboprops and flying fixed schedules to keep the price closer to commercial while matching the private-jet experience</cite>. Two things stand out:

- **Utilization discipline mattered.** Surf Air deliberately <cite index="8-1">capped membership to guarantee there were always open seats for last-minute bookings and to protect service quality</cite>, rather than overselling capacity.
- **The model didn't stay static.** Surf Air <cite index="5-1">cycled through multiple business models over a decade, moving from owning its own aircraft to an "asset-light" outsourced charter model</cite>, largely because owning and operating aircraft directly is extremely capital-intensive and margin-thin. A Surf Air commercial planning executive noted the appeal of the subscription model was specifically <cite index="6-1">predictable, recurring revenue — something traditional commercial carriers don't have</cite> — but predictable revenue alone didn't solve the underlying capital intensity of owning planes.

**Implication for the Mutual:** owning aircraft is the hardest, most capital-intensive part of this business. Surf Air's pivot away from full ownership is a strong signal that the Aviation Mutual should **not** start by owning aircraft (see Phased Roadmap, §8) — it should start asset-light and only acquire aircraft once member volume can support the utilization needed to make ownership pencil out.

### 4.2 NetJets / Flexjet — fractional ownership discipline
NetJets remains <cite index="15-1">the global pioneer of fractional ownership, backed by Berkshire Hathaway, with one of the largest private fleets in the world, and is the benchmark for very heavy travelers despite requiring a substantial financial commitment</cite>. Its model works because Berkshire's balance sheet absorbs the capital intensity that broke smaller competitors. Industry analysis suggests <cite index="12-1">fractional ownership makes the most financial sense in the 50–400 flight-hour-per-year band — below that, on-demand charter is cheaper, and above it, whole-aircraft ownership wins</cite>.

**Implication for the Mutual:** don't design the model around light users. The Mutual's economics need a critical mass of members who fly frequently enough that shared aircraft ownership beats them chartering individually — otherwise the Mutual is just an expensive charter broker.

### 4.3 Wheels Up — membership without full asset ownership, and why it needed a bailout
Wheels Up <cite index="12-1">launched with a membership model rather than pure fractional ownership, and members access aircraft through a mix of owned fleet, fractional aircraft, and charter partners</cite>. It scaled fast but needed <cite index="9-1">a Delta-led recapitalization to stabilize</cite> — evidence that a membership-charter hybrid without disciplined unit economics can burn through capital quickly even with a large member base.

**Implication for the Mutual:** growth for its own sake is dangerous. The Mutual's not-for-profit structure removes the pressure to chase growth for investor returns, but it must still hit break-even utilization on each aircraft before adding the next one, or it risks the same capital trap.

### 4.4 Credit unions / mutual insurers — the governance model to copy
The reason mutuals work in banking and insurance isn't just "no profit motive" — it's disciplined reserve requirements, regulatory oversight, and conservative capital management. The Aviation Mutual should adopt the same posture: mandatory reserves before any member rebate, and no dues cut that isn't sustainable multiple years out.

## 5. Comparable Pricing Reference

| Model | Entry cost | Recurring cost | Hourly flight cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| NetJets fractional (light jet, 1/16th) | ~$850,000+ | $12,000–$15,000/mo management fee | ~$8,500/hr |
| Wheels Up membership | ~$100,000 prefunded deposit + $8,500/yr fee | — | ~$6,000–$7,800/hr |
| Surf Air (historical, regional) | $1,000 signup | $1,750/mo | Included (all-you-can-fly, fixed routes) |
| **Aviation Mutual (target)** | Modest/no signup fee | TBD monthly dues | At-cost, no shareholder margin |

The Mutual's competitive edge is not "cheaper than commercial" — it will never be. Its edge is **cheaper than NetJets/Wheels Up/VistaJet for a comparable experience**, because there's no profit layer sitting between subscription dollars and flight-hour cost.

## 6. Financial Model — Assumptions & Structure

> These are planning assumptions to be validated with real quotes, not final figures.

### 6.1 Revenue
- Monthly membership dues (tiered by booking priority/flight volume expectations)
- Optional one-time joining fee (helps fund initial reserve, discourages low-commitment signups)

### 6.2 Cost structure (per aircraft, illustrative)
| Cost category | Notes |
|---|---|
| Aircraft acquisition/lease | Largest single capital item; delay until utilization is proven (see roadmap) |
| Fuel | Variable with flight hours |
| Maintenance | Scheduled (predictable) + unscheduled (reserve required) |
| Crew (pilots, ground staff) | Fixed cost, scales in steps as fleet grows |
| Insurance | Fixed, but sensitive to fleet size and safety record |
| Hangar/airport fees | Fixed per base airport |
| Admin/ops/software | Booking platform, member services, finance/compliance |

### 6.3 Reserve Fund (Non-Negotiable)
Every mutual/cooperative that has failed to maintain adequate reserves has failed the organization, not just underperformed. The Mutual must maintain:
- A maintenance reserve (per aircraft, per flight hour) sized to unscheduled maintenance events
- A minimum operating reserve (e.g., 3–6 months of fixed costs) before any surplus is distributed to members

### 6.4 Break-even logic
Break-even depends on: (a) cost per flight hour at a given utilization rate, versus (b) dues revenue per member. This must be modeled with real aircraft-lease and charter-rate quotes before any capital commitment — the model should not assume Surf Air's or Wheels Up's published numbers apply directly, since those are for-profit companies with different cost structures.

## 7. Risks

| Risk | Why it matters | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Capital intensity of aircraft ownership** | Broke or forced pivots at Surf Air; requires deep reserves | Start asset-light (charter/lease), only buy once utilization is proven |
| **Utilization risk** | Empty seats = pure cost with no offsetting revenue | Cap membership relative to capacity, as Surf Air did; use InstaFlight (see companion plan) to fill excess capacity |
| **Regulatory (FAA Part 91 vs Part 135)** | Structure affects what "shared ownership + shared use" is legally allowed to look like | Aviation regulatory counsel required before finalizing legal structure |
| **Governance failure** | Member-owned structures fail without real financial discipline and transparency | Independent board, audited financials, mandatory reserve policy |
| **Slow membership growth** | Fixed costs (crew, hangar) exist even at low membership | Phase in fleet size strictly behind membership growth, not ahead of it |
| **Member expectation mismatch** | Members expecting NetJets-level flexibility on a cooperative budget will be disappointed | Set expectations clearly at signup: this is a cooperative, not a luxury concierge service |

## 8. Phased Roadmap

### Phase 1 — Prove Demand Without Owning Aircraft
- Launch as a membership program that charters flights on behalf of members, pooling their collective volume for better group rates
- Build the reservation platform and member base
- Use this phase to gather real utilization and route-demand data

### Phase 2 — Negotiate Volume Pricing
- Use aggregated member volume to negotiate preferred rates with charter operators
- Begin formal cooperative governance structure (board, bylaws, audited books)

### Phase 3 — First Aircraft Acquisition
- Once a specific route/aircraft type shows sustained, provable demand at a utilization rate that beats charter economics, acquire (lease or buy) the first aircraft
- Fund via member reserve/capital contribution, not debt that outpaces the reserve fund

### Phase 4 — Fleet & Route Expansion
- Add aircraft only behind demonstrated demand, one at a time
- Expand destinations based on member request patterns, not speculative growth

### Phase 5 — Mature Network
- Multiple aircraft, multiple bases, established governance and reserve track record
- Consider whether excess capacity should be offered to non-members via a companion access layer (see the InstaFlight companion plan) to improve utilization without diluting member ownership
